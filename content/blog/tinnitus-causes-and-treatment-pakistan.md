Ringing in the ears is one of the most common complaints in an audiology clinic, and one of the most misunderstood. Some people arrive convinced they have a brain tumour. Others have lived with the noise for years and assume nothing can be done.

Both are usually wrong. Here is what tinnitus is, what causes it, when it needs urgent attention, and what genuinely helps.

## What tinnitus actually is

Tinnitus is the perception of sound without an external source. It is a symptom, not a disease, and it can sound like ringing, hissing, buzzing, clicking, or a distant hum. It may be constant or come and go, one ear or both, quiet enough to ignore or loud enough to disturb sleep.

It is very common. Most adults experience it briefly at some point, often after a loud event, and most cases are not dangerous. What matters is the pattern and whether it comes with other symptoms.

## The two types

**Subjective tinnitus** is what almost everyone has: only you can hear it. It comes from the hearing system itself — most often the auditory pathway reacting to some degree of hearing loss.

**Objective tinnitus** is rare and audible to an examiner with a stethoscope. It is usually related to blood flow near the ear or to muscle spasms, which is why pulsatile tinnitus — a rhythm that matches your heartbeat — always deserves a medical assessment.

## Common causes

| Cause | What it looks like |
| :--- | :--- |
| Hearing loss (any degree) | Constant ringing, often high-pitched, both ears, gradual onset |
| Noise exposure | Ringing after concerts, weddings, machinery, or loud headphones |
| Earwax blockage | Muffled hearing and ringing on one side, resolves once cleared |
| Ear infection or fluid | Pain, pressure, reduced hearing, recent cold |
| Some medications | Onset after starting a new drug; ask your doctor, never stop a prescription on your own |
| Jaw (TMJ) problems | Clicking, one-sided, worse when you clench or chew |
| Blood pressure, anaemia, thyroid | Pulsatile or worsening with general health changes |
| Stress, poor sleep, anxiety | Louder in quiet rooms and at night, improves with rest |

The first item is the one people skip. Most persistent tinnitus comes with some measurable hearing loss, even mild loss the person has not noticed — which is why a hearing test is the first useful step rather than the last.

## When to see a doctor rather than wait

See an ENT specialist or audiologist promptly if you have:

- **Tinnitus in one ear only.** One-sided symptoms need examination.
- **Pulsatile tinnitus** — a sound that keeps time with your heartbeat.
- **Sudden hearing loss with the ringing.** This is a medical emergency; treatment within 72 hours matters.
- Discharge, pain, or a feeling of fullness in the ear.
- Dizziness, vertigo, or balance problems alongside it.
- Tinnitus that started after a head injury.
- Ringing that is getting steadily worse, or is severe enough to affect sleep, mood, or work.

For everything else — gradual, both ears, no other symptoms — a hearing test and a routine appointment are the right first step, not an emergency.

## What actually helps

There is no single cure, and anyone selling one is selling something. What works is managing the noise until the brain stops treating it as a threat.

**1. Treat any hearing loss.** This is the strongest lever. When someone has both tinnitus and hearing loss, fitting hearing aids reduces the tinnitus for a large share of people, because the brain receives the sound input it was missing. It is not guaranteed, but it is the most reliable improvement available. See [what a hearing test involves](/blog/hearing-test-in-pakistan-what-to-expect).

**2. Sound enrichment.** Quiet rooms make tinnitus louder, because the brain turns up its own internal noise in the absence of sound. Low-level background sound — a fan, white noise, gentle rain, a radio at low volume — reduces the contrast and helps sleep. Sound therapy is not a cure; it is a tool for making the noise less prominent.

**3. Habituation, usually with CBT.** The goal of most modern treatment is not silence but irrelevance: the brain learns to stop flagging the sound. Cognitive behavioural therapy has the best evidence base here, and it helps even when the tinnitus itself does not change. Counselling plus sound is better than either alone.

**4. Sleep and stress, treated seriously.** Tinnitus and poor sleep feed each other. Fixing sleep often reduces reported loudness. Alcohol, caffeine, and late nights make it worse for many people; it is worth testing on yourself rather than assuming.

**5. Protect your ears from here on.** Earplugs in genuinely loud environments, lower headphone volume, and distance from generators and traffic noise. Preventing further damage protects what hearing you have.

## What does not work

Ginkgo biloba, zinc and magnesium supplements, "tinnitus drops", and most herbal remedies have shown no reliable benefit in trials. Some people feel better taking them, which is worth acknowledging, but they are not treatments and they should not delay the things that are: a hearing test, hearing aids if you need them, and CBT if the noise is affecting your life.

Be sceptical of any clinic offering a guaranteed cure, a one-off laser, or a treatment plan that starts before your hearing has been measured.

## Living with it in Pakistan

Two local realities shape tinnitus here. Traffic, generators, and construction noise mean many people accumulate noise damage without noticing; ear protection in those environments is not an overreaction. And weddings, concerts, and loudspeaker-heavy gatherings are common single-event triggers — if your ears ring or feel dull afterwards, that is damage, not just fatigue, and it can be cumulative.
